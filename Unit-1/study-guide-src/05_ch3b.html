3.6Data Science@@s3-6@@

Definition

Data science is the science of extracting knowledge from data. In simple words, it finds useful and hidden patterns in large amounts of data. It uses ideas from mathematics, statistics and information technology, and also machine learning, data engineering, probability models, statistical learning and pattern recognition.

📌 Simple example — student exam marks
DATARaw marks of students A–E in Maths, Science, English
→
PATTERNScience marks are highest on average; English is lowest
→
KNOWLEDGEStudents do well in Science but need more support in English
→
DECISIONPlan extra English practice to improve overall performance

Data Science turns data into knowledge and helps us make better decisions.

Where is Data Science used?

🌦 Weather predictions

Use past & current weather data to predict future weather.

🛢 Oil drilling

Study geological & sensor data to support drilling operations.

🌍 Seismic activities

Analyse earth-movement data to understand earthquakes.

💳 Financial frauds

Detect unusual transactions and suspicious behaviour.

👍 Social media analytics

Study posts, likes and trends to understand users.

🛒 Retail & manufacturing

Customer churn, market basket analysis, filtering & regression.

Skill 1 — Business acumen

Business acumen means understanding the real problem and the needs of the organisation. "It's not just about data, it's about real-world impact."

1 · Understanding of domain

Know the field: banking, healthcare, education, retail, etc. Each industry has different problems and needs.

2 · Business strategy

Understand what the organisation wants: increase profit, improve service, reach more customers, reduce cost.

3 · Problem solving

Convert a business problem into a solvable data problem. "Why are customers leaving?" → analyse customer data to find patterns of churn.

4 · Communication

Explain ideas clearly to others; make complex ideas simple.

5 · Presentation

Show results using simple reports, charts and slides — a good visual helps everyone understand.

6 · Inquisitiveness

Keep asking: Why? What happened? What can we improve? Can we do better?

3.7Data Scientist & responsibilities@@s3-7@@

Who is a data scientist?

A data scientist is a person who works with data to solve real-world problems. Raw data is often messy; the data scientist prepares the data, studies it, finds patterns and explains what they mean. The final goal is not just a graph or a model — it is a useful decision.

A · Raw datamarks, sales, clicks, sensor readings, customer records
→
B · Analysisclean the data, compare values, find patterns
→
C · Insightexplain what the data tells us and what action to take

Main responsibilities of a data scientist (4)

Figure 3.8 — Data scientist: your new best friend!
🗄 1. Data management

Raw data must be prepared before we can learn from it.

RAW (marks from different classes; values missing/repeated) → CLEAN (correct errors, remove duplicates, handle missing values) → INTEGRATE (combine marks, attendance, other info) → READY (one clean dataset to analyse).

🔍 2. Analytical techniques

Use models and algorithms to understand relationships, spot trends, uncover patterns. The technique depends on the question and the type of data. A model is useful only when it answers the actual question.

Q: Which students may need extra support? → Analyse marks + attendance → Result: support students earlier.

🎯 3. Business / domain understanding

Numbers alone are not enough — domain knowledge turns a statistical result into a meaningful solution.

Cool fact: "Attendance and marks are related." → Business insight: "Students with repeated low attendance may need early mentoring." → Useful action: mentoring, reminders, extra support.

💬 4. Communication & presentation

A model may produce hundreds of numbers. Convert it into a chart, key message and simple language so teachers, managers or customers can act.

Complex analysis → Simple explanation → Stakeholder decision. A good data scientist must be a good presenter and communicator too.

1. In-memory analytics

🗣 In simple words
Reading from a hard disk is like walking to the library every time you need a fact. In-memory analytics keeps the books on your desk (RAM) — so answers come much faster.
  • Data access from non-volatile storage such as hard disk is a slow process. The more data is fetched from hard disk/secondary storage, the slower the process gets.
  • One way to combat this: pre-process and store data (cubes, aggregate tables, query sets, etc.) so the CPU fetches only a small subset of records. But this requires thinking in advance about what data will be needed.
  • If different or more data is needed, it's back to pre-computing or fetching from secondary storage.
  • This problem is solved by in-memory analytics: all the relevant data is stored in RAM (primary storage), eliminating the need to access data from hard disk.
🔑 Advantages of in-memory analytics
Faster access · rapid deployment · better (quicker) insights · minimal IT involvement (less dependence on repeated disk access).

2. In-database processing

  • Also called in-database analytics. It works by fusing data warehouses with analytical systems.
  • Typically, data from various enterprise OLTP (On-Line Transaction Processing) systems — after cleaning up (de-duplication, scrubbing, etc.) through ETL — is stored in the Enterprise Data Warehouse (EDW) or data marts.
  • Without it: the huge datasets are then exported to analytical programs for complex and extensive computations.
  • With in-database processing: the database program itself can run the computations, eliminating the need for export and thereby saving time. Leading database vendors offer this to large businesses.
OLTP systemsoperational data
→
ETLclean / de-duplicate / scrub
→
EDW / Data martstore prepared data
→
Analytics inside the DBno export needed

ETL = Extract, Transform, Load · EDW = Enterprise Data Warehouse

💡 Remember the pair
In-memory → brings data closer to the CPU (RAM instead of disk). In-database → brings computation to where the data lives (no export). Both reduce data movement to save time.

3. Symmetric Multiprocessor System (SMP)

  • In SMP there is a single common main memory shared by two or more identical processors.
  • The processors have full access to all I/O devices and are controlled by a single operating system instance.
  • SMPs are tightly coupled multiprocessor systems. Each processor has its own high-speed memory, called cache memory, and they are connected using a system bus.
Figure 3.9 — Symmetric Multiprocessor System

4. Massively Parallel Processing (MPP)

  • MPP refers to the coordinated processing of programs by a number of processors working in parallel.
  • The processors each have their own operating system and dedicated memory. They work on different parts of the same program.
  • MPP processors communicate using some sort of messaging interface.
  • MPP systems are more difficult to program: the application must be divided so that all executing segments can communicate with each other.
  • MPP differs from SMP: SMP processors share the same OS and same memory; SMP is also called tightly-coupled multiprocessing.
Figure 3.10 — Massively Parallel Processing
FeatureSMPMPP
MemoryCommon / shared main memoryEach node has dedicated memory
CouplingTightly coupledLooser than SMP; nodes communicate
Operating systemSingle OS instanceEach processor/node has its own OS
CommunicationShared memory / system busMessaging between nodes
ProgrammingEasierHarder — program must be divided into communicating segments
🧠 Memory trick
SMP = "Same Memory, Please" — everyone shares one memory & one OS. MPP = "My Private Partition" — each processor has its own memory & OS.

5. Parallel systems vs distributed systems

Parallel systems

  • A parallel database system is a tightly coupled system. The processors co-operate for query processing.
  • The user is unaware of the parallelism since he/she has no access to a specific processor of the system.
  • Either the processors have access to a common memory or make use of message passing for communication.
Figure 3.11 — Parallel system

Distributed systems

  • Distributed database systems are loosely coupled and composed of individual machines.
  • Each machine can run its own application and serve its own users.
  • Data is usually distributed across several machines, so quite a number of machines may need to be accessed to answer a user query.
Figure 3.12 — Distributed system
Parallel systemDistributed system
CouplingTightly coupledLoosely coupled
Made ofProcessors cooperating on the same problem (e.g., one large query)Separate machines connected by a network
Memory/communicationCommon memory or message passingNetwork messages; data spread across machines
User's viewUser sees one system; unaware of parallelismEach machine runs its own app and serves its own users; a query may contact several nodes

6. Shared nothing architecture

The three most common types of architecture for multiprocessor, high-transaction-rate systems:

Figure 3.13 — Three multiprocessor architectures
  • Shared Memory (SM): a common central memory is shared by multiple processors.
  • Shared Disk (SD): multiple processors share a common collection of disks while having their own private memory. Coordination is needed when many nodes access shared data.
  • Shared Nothing (SN): neither memory nor disk is shared among processors. Each node has its own processor, memory and storage; nodes communicate through the network.

Advantages of a "Shared Nothing Architecture"

🛡 1. Fault isolation

A fault in a single node is contained and confined to that node exclusively and exposed only through messages (or lack of it). It doesn't spread through a common memory/disk.

📈 2. Scalability

If the disk is a shared resource, the controller and disk bandwidth are also shared. Synchronisation is needed to keep a consistent shared state, so nodes must take turns to access critical data — this limits how many nodes can be added. In SN nothing is shared → add nodes freely, no central bottleneck.

🎯 Exam tip
Hadoop/HDFS (Unit 2!) uses a shared-nothing architecture — each data node has its own CPU, memory and disk. Mention this link in answers; it shows understanding.

3.9CAP theorem@@s3-9@@

Definition

The CAP theorem is also called Brewer's Theorem. It states that in a distributed computing environment (a collection of interconnected nodes that share data), it is impossible to provide all three guarantees — Consistency, Availability and Partition tolerance — at the same time. At best you can have two of the three — one must be sacrificed.

C · Consistency

Every read fetches the last write (latest data).

A · Availability

Reads and writes always succeed: each non-failing node returns a response in a reasonable amount of time.

P · Partition tolerance

The system continues to function when a network partition (communication break between nodes) occurs.

Figure 3.14 — Databases and CAP

Understanding CAP with a story

Figure 3.15 — Scenario: consistency
  • Availability: if an administrator is working, the user should get a response in a reasonable time. Problem: if we force both administrators to synchronise before every response, users may have to wait.
  • Partition tolerance: suppose Raju and Rahul cannot communicate because of a network problem. A partition-tolerant system continues to operate despite this communication break.
C · Choose Consistency during a partition

Stop or delay some operations until nodes agree → correct/latest data, but some requests may not be served immediately.

A · Choose Availability during a partition

Keep answering from reachable nodes → requests get responses, but some responses may temporarily use older data.

🔑 Key point
Network partitions will happen in a distributed system, so P is usually a must. The real choice during a partition is Consistency vs Availability.

CAP combinations

CombinationMeaningExamples (textbook figure)
CAConsistency + Availability: up-to-date reads and responses while there is no network partition to handleTraditional RDBMS — PostgreSQL, MySQL
CPConsistency + Partition tolerance: during a partition, prefer correct/consistent data even if some requests must wait or failHBase, MongoDB, Redis, MemcacheDB, BigTable-like systems
APAvailability + Partition tolerance: during a partition, keep the system responsive, accepting that replicas may temporarily disagreeRiak, Cassandra, CouchDB, Dynamo-like systems
Prefer CONSISTENCY when…
  • Incorrect or stale data can cause serious problems.
  • Reads and writes must reflect the latest confirmed state.

e.g., bank balance, critical inventory update.
Ask: "Can I tolerate an old value?" → No → choose C.

Prefer AVAILABILITY when…
  • The service must keep responding even during network problems.
  • Temporary differences between replicas can be corrected later.

e.g., social feeds, caches, highly available services.
Ask: "Can I tolerate temporary inconsistency?" → Yes → choose A.

🧠 Memory trick — which DB is where?
CA = "Classic Apps" → old RDBMS (MySQL, PostgreSQL).
CP = "Correct Please" → H M R M B: "Happy Monkeys Run Many Bananas" — HBase, MongoDB, Redis, MemcacheDB, BigTable.
AP = "Always Present" → R C C D: "Real Cats Chase Dogs" — Riak, Cassandra, CouchDB, Dynamo.
